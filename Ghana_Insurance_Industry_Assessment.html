Executive Assessment

  • There is a real, fundable opportunity for a Ghana-first insurance ERP, but it is primarily a "greenfield/manual-replacement" play among small-to-mid-size insurers rather than a rip-and-replace of global platforms — even the state-owned SIC Life admits its claims delays stem from the "manual system under which the company operates" (David Jockic Ankrah, Head of Corporate/Group Business, SIC Life), and Deloitte's Africa Insurance Outlook 2025/2026 cites "outdated systems, poor data quality, and a shortage of skilled actuarial professionals" as core barriers to IFRS 17 compliance.
  • Regulatory tailwinds are strong and specific: mandatory IFRS 17 reporting is now live, the NIC requires a non-outsourceable in-house actuarial function (Section 77, Act 1061), a 150% capital-adequacy ratio, and a risk-based capital (RBC) framework scheduled for 2026 — all of which demand exactly the data infrastructure most Ghanaian insurers lack.
  • The market is small (~49 insurers, ~1% penetration) and concentrated at the top, but fragmented in the middle/tail, where dozens of mid-tier and newly recapitalized insurers, 100+ brokers, and ~12,500 agents run on spreadsheets or aging point solutions — the affordable, IFRS-17-ready, locally-supported white space that global vendors (Guidewire, Duck Creek, Sapiens, Majesco, EIS) do not serve.

Key Findings

1. Regulator (NIC). Ghana's insurance sector is regulated by the National Insurance Commission under the Insurance Act, 2021 (Act 1061), which replaced the 2006 Act. Act 1061 created three licence categories — Insurance Licence, Reinsurance Licence, and a new Innovative Insurance Licence — plus separate licensing regimes for intermediaries (brokers, agents, loss adjusters). It added three compulsory lines (public liability, professional indemnity, marine cargo) on top of existing compulsory motor and commercial-building fire cover, and mandates NIC approval for significant owners and key control persons. The current Acting Commissioner is Dr. Abiba Zakariah (per the Bank of Ghana Financial Stability Review 2025).

2. Capital & solvency. The minimum capital requirement, set in June 2019 and enforced from 31 December 2021, is GHS 50 million for life and non-life insurers (up from GHS 15m), GHS 125 million for reinsurers, GHS 500,000 for broking companies/loss adjusters, and GHS 1 million for reinsurance brokers. Insurers must also maintain a 150% capital adequacy ratio. No new flat capital increase has been announced for 2025–2027; instead the NIC is moving to risk-based capital — a life RBC framework announced by then-Acting Commissioner Michael Kofi Andoh in July 2024, and an industry-wide RBC framework the NIC has stated is "scheduled for implementation in 2026."

3. IFRS 17 & reporting. Ghana has transitioned from IFRS 4 to IFRS 17; the first IFRS 17-compliant reports have been submitted and are under NIC review. Per Deloitte's Africa Insurance Outlook 2025/2026 (Ghana section by Kwabena Situ, Partner, and Richard Omari, Associate Director, Deloitte Africa), implementation has "exposed significant gaps, including outdated systems, poor data quality, and a shortage of skilled actuarial professionals," with per-firm implementation cost "estimated at about $110,000 and remained a major concern, particularly for smaller firms." Insurers file unaudited quarterly returns and audited annual statements, must run an in-house (non-outsourceable) actuarial function under Section 77 of Act 1061, and the NIC is building an Assured Life Mortality Table and non-life actuarial capacity via the UNDP-Milliman GAIN programme.

4. Market size & structure. Gross written premium reached GHS 6.1 billion (~$738m) in 2022 and the sector grew 232% between 2018 and 2022. According to the Financial Stability Council's Financial Stability Review (reported by Ghana News Agency, 20 May 2026), the industry recorded real revenue growth of 19.9% in 2025 — "markedly higher than the 7.6 per cent achieved in 2024" — with life insurers retaining 95.3% of premiums in 2025. Penetration remains ~1% based on gross premiums; under the IFRS 17 Insurance Service Revenue metric, 2024 penetration was lower at 0.63% (Bank of Ghana 2024 Financial Stability Review) — far below South Africa (~17%). As of September 2023 there were 49 insurers (28 non-life, 21 life), 4 reinsurers, 5 reinsurance brokers, 116 brokerages, and ~12,500 agents (Oxford Business Group). The life segment is concentrated (top three — StarLife, Enterprise Life, SIC Life — held ~61% of assets in 2023); non-life is less concentrated (top three ~38%). Deloitte's 2025/26 outlook states there are "about 50 licensed insurers and reinsurers."

5. Consolidation & entrants. The 2019–2021 recapitalization drove consolidation and license-revocation threats. Recent M&A/entry activity includes the SanlamAllianz merger (final NIC no-objection May 2024, part of a 27-country, ~$2bn joint venture), emPLE Group's acquisition of Metropolitan Ghana from South Africa's Momentum, StarHealth Insurance's launch, and government plans to list part of Ghana Re on the stock exchange. The Domestic Debt Exchange Programme (DDEP) wiped out much of insurers' holdings — "prior to the debt restructuring, insurers held approximately GH¢4.6 billion in government securities" (Deloitte) — prompting a $750m Financial Stability Fund; inflation reached 23.8% in December 2024.

6. Technology landscape. Global tier-1 platforms (Guidewire, Duck Creek, Sapiens, Majesco, EIS) have essentially no footprint in Ghana — Guidewire's first African client was Santam (South Africa) in 2012. The Ghanaian/regional vendor field is thin: Turnkey Africa (Kenya-based, TurnQuest suite; deployed GN Life Ghana 2016, Liberty, Pioneer), Agile Financial Technologies (Agilis, deployed Esich Life Ghana), and insurtech infrastructure players like Curacel (Nigeria; claims/fraud APIs, active in Ghana) and ETAP. ETAP secured Ghana's first NIC insurtech operational licence in October 2024, operating as "ETAP-Hollard" in partnership with Hollard Insurance Ghana (CEO Ibraheem Babalola; ETAP raised a $1.5m pre-seed in 2022). Local software house SOFTtribe exists but is not insurance-specialized. Many insurers still rely on manual/spreadsheet processes; SIC Life said it would complete full digital transition only by Q1 2026, targeting claims payment "within ten minutes" once digital.

7. NIC digitization push. The NIC has actively pushed technology: a regulatory sandbox (first cohort of five insurtechs — Figtech, Moovon Insure, Hollard/ETAP, Trade Guarantee, Red Pear — approved October 2024, backed by FSD Africa's BimaLab), an Innolab insurtech accelerator (GIZ/FSD Africa/UKaid funded), a Motor Insurance Database (MID), and a nearly-complete Marine & Aviation Insurance Database (MAID). Andoh described promoting technology as "a key pillar in the current strategic plan of the NIC."

Details

1. Regulatory landscape

The NIC's mandate under Act 1061 covers licensing, standards-setting, premium-rate and commission approval, complaints resolution, and claims arbitration. Act 1061 modernized the 2006 framework: three licence categories (Insurance, Reinsurance, Innovative Insurance), separation of life and non-life underwriting into distinct legal entities, NIC pre-approval of significant owners and key persons, and expanded compulsory lines. Unlicensed insurance business is punishable by a maximum GHS 600,000 fine (~$50,000) or five years' imprisonment.

Compliance obligations most relevant to an ERP:

  • Statutory returns: unaudited quarterly submissions and audited annual financial statements to the NIC (which feeds the Financial Stability Council).
  • IFRS 17: now mandatory; revenue/expense recognized as service is delivered; requires granular contract-group data, discounting, risk adjustment, and CSM (contractual service margin) tracking — impractical on spreadsheets at scale.
  • Actuarial function: Section 77 of Act 1061 requires a fully operational in-house actuarial function that may not be outsourced — driving demand for reserving/valuation data pipelines.
  • Capital adequacy: 150% CAR; the RBC framework (life first, then industry-wide in 2026) will require risk-based asset/liability and technical-provision data.
  • Reserving/technical provisions: unearned premium reserves, outstanding claims reserves and IBNR are standard NIC categories under the solvency framework (originally effective January 2015, covering capital resources, capital adequacy, solvency control levels, investments, technical provisions, and valuation of assets and liabilities).

Recent developments (2024–2026): the 15% VAT on non-life premiums (effective 1 July 2025); NIC suspension of the Ghana Insurers Association's proposed 10% motor premium increase (citing consumer burden); the insurtech sandbox and the RBC/mortality-table workstreams; and NIC measures to address chronic underwriting losses.

2. Market structure

GWP: GHS 458m (2010) → GHS 4.2bn (2020) → GHS 6.1bn (2022), roughly 25% ten-year CAGR through 2020. Life overtook non-life in premium around 2020 (GHS 2.0bn life vs GHS 2.2bn non-life in 2020). Motor dominates non-life (>50% of non-oil non-life premium). Distribution: life is agent-led (~61% agents/tied), non-life is broker-led (~44% brokers).

Concentration: top-five non-life insurers held ~48% of assets in 2021; life top-three ~61% in 2023 (StarLife ~25.7%, Enterprise Life ~19.4%, SIC Life ~15.9%). But the tail is long — dozens of mid- and small insurers competing on price, many recently recapitalized and now needing to justify their GHS 50m capital with efficient operations. Key players: Enterprise (Life & Insurance), SIC (Life & Insurance), StarLife/Star Assurance, Hollard, Glico, Ghana Union Assurance, Prudential Life, Old Mutual, Vanguard, Activa, SanlamAllianz, plus reinsurer Ghana Re.

3. Technology landscape & pain points

The evidence points to a technology vacuum in the mid-market:

  • No tier-1 global platforms: Guidewire/Duck Creek/Sapiens/Majesco/EIS are priced and scoped for large multinational carriers; their African footprint is largely South Africa (Santam on Guidewire since 2012).
  • Thin regional vendor field: Turnkey Africa (TurnQuest — life, P&C, bancassurance/brokerage, plus a financial-management ERP module) is the most credible incumbent competitor, with Ghana deployments (GN Life) and pan-African reach. Agile Financial Technologies' Agilis was deployed at Esich Life (which itself later faced claims-payment problems and NIC intervention). These two constitute the direct competitive set for a full core.
  • Insurtech layer, not core ERP: Curacel (claims/fraud APIs), ETAP (embedded motor), MyCover.ai, Octamile, Redpear — these are distribution/claims-automation layers, not full policy-admin + accounting cores, leaving room for a true system-of-record.
  • Manual processes persist: SIC Life's own executive attributed claims delays to a "manual system"; Deloitte cites "outdated systems, poor data quality" as the binding constraint on IFRS 17. Local narratives estimate roughly 70% of Ghanaians lack insurance, partly attributable to operational inefficiencies and low trust.

4. Opportunity assessment

  • Affordability gap is the core wedge: Global cores cost millions to license and implement; the mid-market cannot afford them. IFRS 17 compliance alone is costing ~$110,000 per firm. A locally-hosted, locally-supported, modular ERP priced for GHS-denominated mid-tier insurers fills a genuine gap.
  • Greenfield-dominant, with displacement upside: The primary opportunity is replacing spreadsheets/manual workflows (greenfield). Secondary opportunity is displacing aging or partial deployments (some TurnQuest/Agilis installs, homegrown systems). This is favorable — greenfield sales avoid painful data-migration battles against entrenched incumbents.
  • Regulatory forcing function: IFRS 17, the in-house actuarial mandate, RBC 2026, and quarterly statutory returns give a compliance-driven "must-buy" narrative — the strongest possible wedge for a vertical ERP.
  • Government/NIC digitization: sandbox, MID/MAID databases, and accelerator programs signal a regulator that welcomes technology and could become a channel/endorser.
  • Playbook analogues: Turnkey Africa (Kenya→pan-African), Curacel (Nigeria→multi-country), and Ethiopia's regulator-mandated core-system adoption (Bunna Insurance selecting Amity after the National Bank of Ethiopia directed insurers to acquire automated systems) show that (a) regulator mandates catalyze core-system buying and (b) a "build in one African market, expand regionally" motion works. The EzFMC OMC precedent (a vertical ERP already built for a regulated Ghanaian industry) is directly transferable in method — same regulator-driven compliance dynamics, same buyer sophistication tier.

5. Product scope considerations

General/non-life vs life differences that shape design:

  • Non-life (motor, fire, marine, engineering, liability): short-tail, high-volume, policy/renewal-heavy; unearned-premium-reserve and outstanding-claims/IBNR reserving; heavy broker commission accounting; reinsurance treaty/facultative cessions; and compulsory-line reporting (MID/MAID integration).
  • Life: long-tail; actuarial reserves (mathematical/policyholder liabilities); mortality-table-based pricing (the NIC's forthcoming Assured Life Mortality Table); surrender/maturity/annuity processing; and heavier actuarial-valuation and IFRS 17 (CSM, risk adjustment) machinery.

Core modules needed:

  • Product configuration & underwriting (rules, rating engine)
  • Premium calculation & billing (aligned to "No Premium, No Cover")
  • Policy administration (issuance, endorsements, renewals, cancellations)
  • Claims administration & adjudication workflows
  • Commission/intermediary management (agents, brokers, tiered structures)
  • Reinsurance treaty & facultative management (cessions, recoveries)
  • Actuarial reserving & valuation feeds (UPR, OCR, IBNR, life reserves)
  • Insurance-specific finance/accounting + IFRS 17 sub-ledger
  • Statutory/regulatory reporting (NIC quarterly/annual returns, RBC)
  • Digital channels/APIs (customer/agent portals, MID/MAID, mobile-money payments)

Why insurance finance ≠ generic ERP finance: A general ERP GL cannot natively handle unearned premium reserve amortization, claims reserve movements (IBNR/OCR), deferred acquisition costs, commission accruals/clawbacks, reinsurance recoverables, or IFRS 17 measurement models (GMM/PAA, CSM roll-forward, risk adjustment). These require insurance-specific sub-ledgers feeding the GL — the central architectural reason a vertical insurance ERP wins over configuring SAP/Oracle/a generic ERP. This is also the exact conceptual extension of a finance-centric ERP like EzFMC into an insurance vertical.

Recommendations

Stage 1 (0–9 months) — Beachhead in non-life mid-market + compliance wedge. Target 3–5 recently-recapitalized mid-tier non-life insurers currently on spreadsheets/partial systems. Lead with the compliance pain: IFRS 17 sub-ledger + NIC statutory returns + motor (MID integration) as the minimum viable core, plus policy admin, claims, commissions, and an insurance-aware GL. Price in GHS well below the ~$110k IFRS 17 project cost and far below global cores. Benchmark to advance: 2 signed pilots and 1 production go-live producing a clean quarterly NIC return directly from the system.

Stage 2 (9–24 months) — Add life + reinsurance + agent/broker portals. Build the actuarial-reserving feeds and life-specific modules (leveraging the NIC Assured Life Mortality Table once released), reinsurance treaty management, and digital portals with mobile-money payment integration. Pursue an explicit relationship with the NIC (sandbox alumni network, accelerator) to become a recognized compliance-reporting vendor. Benchmark: 8–12 Ghanaian insurer clients; at least 2 life insurers live on IFRS 17.

Stage 3 (24–48 months) — Regionalize. Expand to Nigeria (leveraging NAICOM's 2025 insurtech guidelines and the Nigerian Insurance Industry Reform Act 2025), then Francophone West Africa and East Africa, competing directly with Turnkey Africa on price, IFRS 17-readiness, and local support. Benchmark: first non-Ghana deployment and a repeatable multi-country deployment template.

Thresholds that would change the plan:

  • If the NIC mandates a specific core-system standard or endorses a vendor (as Ethiopia's central bank effectively did), accelerate and align to that spec immediately.
  • If Turnkey Africa or a global vendor launches an aggressively-priced Ghana mid-market SKU, shift from head-to-head to niche (e.g., microinsurance/inclusive insurance, or a deep IFRS 17 add-on that integrates with incumbents).
  • If RBC 2026 slips materially, the compliance urgency softens — lean harder on operational-efficiency ROI (claims cycle time, commission leakage).
  • If GHS depreciation/inflation resurges, emphasize local-currency pricing and cloud/opex models over capex.

Caveats

  • Data currency: The most precise entity-count breakdown is from September 2023 (Oxford Business Group); Deloitte's 2025/26 "about 50 licensed insurers and reinsurers" is explicitly rounded and combines insurers with reinsurers. A definitive current segment-by-segment count (especially loss adjusters and agents) should be confirmed from the NIC 2024 Annual Report or Bank of Ghana Financial Stability Review 2024/2025, whose entity tables could not be fully extracted for this report.
  • Reserving line-item specifics (verbatim NIC directive wording on UPR/OCR/IBNR) should be confirmed directly from the NIC's current Solvency & Technical Provisions directive; sourcing on these derives partly from the 2015 solvency framework and secondary reporting.
  • Market-size figures mix GWP (GHS 6.1bn, 2022) with older/other-basis numbers; the IFRS 17 restatement changes the penetration metric (1% on gross premiums vs 0.63% on IFRS 17 Insurance Service Revenue in 2024), so pre- and post-IFRS-17 figures are not directly comparable.
  • Competitive intelligence on incumbents (exact number of TurnQuest/Agilis installs in Ghana, pricing) is limited to vendor press releases; primary validation via customer interviews is recommended before committing.
  • Some 2025–2026 figures (e.g., 19.9% real revenue growth, 95.3% life retention) come from the Financial Stability Council/GNA reporting rather than audited NIC accounts and may be provisional.
